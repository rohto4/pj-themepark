# Theme-park experience research: transferable principles for ASTERIA

Status: raw research record, not a product decision  
Question type: evidence-backed design synthesis  
Researched and accessed: 2026-08-01 JST  
Scope: principles behind memorable physical theme-park journeys, translated cautiously to an original browser-native park. This record does not authorize copying characters, attraction layouts, themed lands, trade dress, music, language, or other protected expression.

## Evidence boundary

- **Sourced fact** means the statement is supported by the opened source named in brackets.
- **Inference** means a design conclusion that follows from one or more sources but was not tested for ASTERIA.
- **Project recommendation** is an actionable proposal for later concept and implementation work; it remains subject to the concept gate and capability evaluation.

## Source ledger

### S1 — Delight is an appraisal, not merely spectacle

- Title: Customer delight from theme park experiences: The Antecedents of Delight based on Cognitive Appraisal Theory
- Authors / publisher: Jianyu Ma, Jun Gao, Noel Scott, and Peiyi Ding; Annals of Tourism Research, Elsevier
- Publication date: July 2013
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annals.2013.02.018
- Opened source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160738313000406
- Exact claim considered: In a survey of 645 tourists leaving a theme park, delight was associated with appraisals that an experience was unexpected, personally important or need-relevant, interesting, or goal-congruent.
- Evidence and limit: This is a physical theme-park study using structural equation modeling, but it establishes associations in one study context rather than a universal causal recipe for every guest.

### S2 — The journey extends beyond the visit

- Title: Theme park experience: Factors explaining amount of pleasure from a theme park visit, time allocation for theme park activities, perceived value, queuing quality, satisfaction, and loyalty
- Authors / publisher: Maksim Godovykh, Ady Milman, and Alsi D. A. Tasci; Journal of Tourism and Leisure Studies; University of Central Florida repository preprint
- Publication date: 2019
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://stars.library.ucf.edu/ucfscholar/946/
- Exact claim considered: The study distinguishes pleasure in anticipation, the visit, and remembering; it reports that prior theme-park experience significantly influenced loyalty and the pleasure received from these phases, and that virtual queuing related to several experience measures.
- Evidence and limit: Repository metadata identifies this as a 2019 preprint. The abstract supports a whole-journey framing, not a claim that any particular finale mechanic will create loyalty.

### S3 — Landmark salience supports wayfinding

- Title: Landmarks in wayfinding: a review of the existing literature
- Authors / publisher: Demet Yesiltepe, Ruth Conroy Dalton, and Ayse Ozbil Torun; Cognitive Processing, Springer Nature
- Publication date: 2021-03-08; issue date August 2021
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10339-021-01012-x
- Opened source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8324579/
- Exact claim considered: Across reviewed real and virtual wayfinding studies, landmarks on-route and at turning decision points are generally more effective; visibility, structural position, distinctiveness, and meaning can all affect salience.
- Evidence and limit: This is a review, largely of urban/outdoor and virtual environments. Landmark usefulness varies with the task, layout, familiarity, and person, so visual distinctiveness alone is not sufficient.

### S4 — Play can reduce perceived queue time, with caveats

- Title: Reducing Perceived Waiting Time in Theme Park Queues via an Augmented Reality Game
- Authors / publisher: Fabio Zambetta and collaborators; ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction; open-access manuscript hosted by Monash University
- Publication date: January 2020
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://doi.org/10.1145/3361524
- Opened source: https://researchmgt.monash.edu/ws/portalfiles/portal/303348242/303347441_oa.pdf
- Exact claim considered: In an in-the-wild comparison at a theme park, 100 players and 100 non-players estimated elapsed queue time differently; the player/non-player difference was statistically significant with a medium effect size, and greater play attention correlated with lower estimated elapsed time.
- Evidence and limit: Participants self-selected after recruitment in a live park and the authors could not isolate player and non-player influence. The reported approximate five-times comparison comes from a simplified regression with strong assumptions, not a general multiplier to promise users.

### S5 — Immersion, not workload alone, is the safer queue-play target

- Title: Interactive Amusement Park Queues: Examining the Indirect Effect of Task Work Load on Guests' Perception of Wait Duration Through Task Immersion
- Author / publisher: Jonathan Ledbetter; University of Central Florida, doctoral dissertation
- Publication date: May 2016
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://stars.library.ucf.edu/etd/4935/
- Exact claim considered: In a virtual-roller-coaster queue experiment, higher game immersion significantly related to lower perceived wait time; a direct workload-to-time-perception effect was not significant, while workload's indirect effect through immersion was significant.
- Evidence and limit: This is an open dissertation and a virtual experiment, not a field comparison of all physical queue designs. It cautions against treating cognitive load itself as the benefit.

### S6 — Agency needs legible, materially different outcomes

- Title: Foreseeing Meaningful Choices
- Authors / publisher: Rogelio E. Cardona-Rivera, Justus Robertson, Stephen G. Ware, Brent Harrison, David L. Roberts, and R. Michael Young; AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Interactive Digital Entertainment
- Publication date: 2014
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical / opened URL: https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12716/12716-52-16233-1-2-20201228.pdf
- Exact claim considered: In an 88-participant choose-your-own-adventure study, choices whose possible outcomes differed in situational content produced higher self-reported agency than choices whose outcomes did not.
- Evidence and limit: This is an interactive-narrative experiment rather than a theme-park field study. It supports discernible local and later feedback, not unlimited branching or punitive consequence.

### S7 — Multisensory cues are not interchangeable or automatically stronger

- Title: Multimodal retrieval of autobiographical memories: sensory information contributes differently to the recollection of events
- Authors / publisher: Johan Willander, Sverker Sikstrom, and Kristina Karlsson; Frontiers in Psychology
- Publication date: 2015-11-05
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01681
- Opened source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01681/full
- Exact claim considered: In a randomized 62-participant study of visual, auditory, olfactory, and multimodal autobiographical-memory cues, multimodal retrieval appeared more influenced by visual and auditory than olfactory information, and the study found no overall differences in number of memories or experiential ratings across the four conditions.
- Evidence and limit: This is a memory-retrieval experiment, not a theme-park study. It is evidence against a simplistic more-senses-equals-more-memory claim, not evidence to omit meaningful sensory design.

### S8 — Small pauses improve well-being more reliably than performance

- Title: Give me a break! A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks for increasing well-being and performance
- Authors / publisher: Patricia Albulescu and collaborators; PLOS ONE
- Publication date: 2022-08-31
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460
- Opened source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/
- Exact claim considered: Across 22 independent experimental or quasi-experimental samples totaling 2,335 people, short breaks had small significant effects on vigor and fatigue, while the overall performance effect was not significant.
- Evidence and limit: The included studies concern work or study tasks, not park visits. The transfer is a pacing hypothesis; it does not prove that a short browser pause will improve completion.

### S9 — Inclusive paths are normative web-quality requirements

- Title: Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2
- Publisher: World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), Accessibility Guidelines Working Group
- Publication / latest W3C Recommendation: 2024-12-12
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/
- Exact claim considered: WCAG 2.2 provides technology-neutral, testable accessibility criteria for web content across desktop and mobile devices, including keyboard operation, visible focus, alternatives for media, controls for moving content, interaction-motion control, and pointer target size.
- Evidence and limit: WCAG is a web standard, not research on theme-park enjoyment. Conformance improves access but does not automatically make an authored experience emotionally strong.

### S10 — Peak and ending are useful hypotheses, not a magic formula

- Title: From Experience to Memory: On the Robustness of the Peak-and-End-Rule for Complex, Heterogeneous Experiences
- Authors / publisher: Wim Strijbosch and collaborators; Frontiers in Psychology
- Publication date: 2019-07-24
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01705
- Opened source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01705/full
- Exact claim considered: In a complex virtual-reality experience, peak and ending valence were inferior to average valence and arousal for predicting remembered experience one week later; the authors conclude the peak-end rule should not be generalized automatically to rich heterogeneous episodes.
- Evidence and limit: This is one VR study, so it does not invalidate all finale design. It directly warns against optimizing only an opening reveal and ending while neglecting the route between them.

### S11 — Friction at the edges can outweigh attraction enjoyment

- Title: What makes a theme park experience less enjoyable? Evidence from online customer reviews of Disneyland China
- Authors / publisher: Shizhen Bai and collaborators; Frontiers in Psychology
- Publication date: 2023-06-29
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1120483
- Opened source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1120483/pdf
- Exact claim considered: Topic and sentiment analysis of 106,072 online reviews of one park found access, service, comparison, and crowd or fast-track-related friction among prominent sources of negative experience; the authors note some service failures can affect overall experience even when guests enjoy attractions.
- Evidence and limit: This is observational review mining from a single named resort and platform. It identifies recurring complaints, not causal effect sizes or universal priorities.

### S12 — Immersion is layered, including sensory and emotional layers

- Title: Theme Park Storytelling: Deconstructing Immersion in Chinese Theme Parks
- Authors / publisher: Xiaoxiao Fu, Carissa A. Baker, Wen Zhang, and Rouyang Zhang; Journal of Travel Research; University of Central Florida repository record
- Publication date: April 2023
- Accessed: 2026-08-01 JST
- Canonical URL: https://doi.org/10.1177/00472875221098933
- Opened source: https://stars.library.ucf.edu/ucfscholar/1195/
- Exact claim considered: Based on interviews with 30 visitors, the authors describe spatial-temporal, sensory, conceptual-imaginative, and emotional immersion as stages in participants' theme-park storytelling experiences.
- Evidence and limit: A qualitative study in Chinese domestic parks; useful for a layered model, not a validated sequence every visitor must follow.

## Principle synthesis

### 1. Emotional arc and reveal

**Sourced facts**

- Delight in a theme-park setting has more than one route: surprise matters, but interest, relevance to the guest, and goal congruence also mattered in S1.
- The park experience can be considered across anticipation, the visit, and remembering rather than as only the ride or finale (S2).
- A rich experience should not be optimized solely around a peak and end because average valence and arousal may predict later memory better (S10).

**Inference**

- A reveal earns its place when it changes what the guest can notice, hope for, or do next. Decorative opacity or a delayed explanation by itself does not create a meaningful emotional arc.
- The strongest ASTERIA arc is likely to use a small initial promise, alternating agency and recovery, accumulating personal traces, and a legible closing reflection rather than a single uninterrupted climb.

**Project recommendation**

- Start with an immediate, tactile, visual response to lighting the star; pair it with one clearly readable next action.
- Reveal the park in layers: a global destination promise first, a small choice next, deeper lore and secrets only after voluntary engagement.
- Define each attraction's intended emotional movement in plain terms such as curiosity to confidence, uncertainty to orientation, or quiet observation to celebration. Test the full route's affect, not just its opening and finale.

### 2. Spatial wayfinding, landmarks, and return paths

**Sourced facts**

- Landmarks work through combinations of visibility, distinctiveness, structural location, and personal or semantic meaning; reviewed evidence especially favors on-route landmarks at decision points (S3).
- Landmark effectiveness varies with the guest's task and familiarity, so no single map treatment works for everyone (S3).

**Inference**

- A browser park can create spatial memory without imitating a real park map: a persistent high-level landmark, one locally distinctive marker per realm, visible return affordances, and a current-location cue should make orientation part of the authored experience.

**Project recommendation**

- Give the park one original sky-scale orientation marker and each realm an original local identifier; use names, silhouette, color, icon, and text rather than color alone.
- At every navigation decision, show three facts without forcing a modal: where the guest is, a promising next destination, and how to return to the central route.
- Offer both exploratory navigation and a short-route guide. Keyboard focus, screen-reader naming, and current-location text must represent the same information as the visual map.

### 3. Convert anticipation and waiting into voluntary play

**Sourced facts**

- In S4's live comparison, playing during a theme-park queue was associated with lower estimated elapsed time; attention to the game correlated with that difference.
- S5 found immersion, not raw workload alone, to be the safer mediator of lower perceived wait in its virtual-queue experiment.
- S4 also reports operational and bodily conflicts: hard-to-see mobile interaction, strain, movement conflicts, and technical failures can undermine the intended benefit.

**Inference**

- In a browser park, loading, travel, and discovery intervals can become short, optional anticipation moments if they tell the guest what is changing and let them do something light. They should never conceal a slow or blocked critical path.

**Project recommendation**

- Replace mandatory loading interludes with optional, interruption-safe micro-play: inspect a detail, tune a motif, choose a route token, or see a stateful preview of the next realm.
- Make progress, duration, skip, pause, mute, and low-power continuation explicit. Do not impose score loss, a timer, camera access, precise dragging, or motion input for core progress.
- Use queue-style content to prepare the next attraction's rule or mystery, not to explain every story beat in advance.

### 4. Agency, consequence, and replay

**Sourced facts**

- In S6, people reported more agency when choice options were perceived to lead to meaningfully different situations; discernible short-term feedback and integrated later feedback are relevant mechanisms.
- The physical-theme-park storytelling literature frames guests as active interpreters of immersion, not merely passive recipients (S12).

**Inference**

- Replay follows from seeing alternate or deepened possibilities, not from multiplying opaque branches. A compact state model can produce strong perceived agency when a guest can recognize their trace soon and later.

**Project recommendation**

- For every core action, specify: the immediate acknowledgement, the persistent state change, the later reappearance, and the finale contribution.
- Keep irreversible loss out of the critical route. Let a guest revisit an attraction, alter an expressive choice, or discover a different variation without erasing their first story.
- Surface enough cause and effect to be legible while retaining optional secrets. A status object such as the star should show change in accessible visual and text form.

### 5. Multisensory memory without sensory dependence

**Sourced facts**

- S12's interview-derived framework includes sensory and emotional layers alongside spatial-temporal and conceptual-imaginative immersion.
- S7 found sensory modalities contributed differently in an autobiographical-memory task; visual and auditory signals appeared more influential than olfactory in its multimodal condition, and adding modalities did not produce an overall memory-count or rating advantage.

**Inference**

- A repeatable motif across visual movement, sound, and interaction may aid coherence, but it should be treated as a signature and recall cue, not as proof of memory engineering.

**Project recommendation**

- Give each realm a small original sensory grammar: a visual rhythm, motion behavior, optional sound texture, and tactile-feeling interaction response. Recombine those grammars in the finale.
- Author a complete mute route with visual representation of timing and state, captions or text equivalents for meaningful audio, and a low-motion route that preserves significance rather than simply disabling content.
- Prefer a few stable motifs over relentless layered effects; test whether guests can describe the difference between realms without audio.

### 6. Inclusive design as part of the attraction, not a side route

**Sourced facts**

- WCAG 2.2 is a current W3C Recommendation with technology-neutral, testable criteria for keyboard functionality, focus, media alternatives, timing and motion control, and target size (S9).
- S9 explicitly notes that even the highest conformance level will not meet every need, especially across cognitive, language, and learning differences.

**Inference**

- An inclusive ASTERIA route should be authored as several equally intentional ways to perceive and act, not a single visual-audio-mouse experience plus retrofitted accommodation.

**Project recommendation**

- Treat keyboard, touch, mouse, reduced motion, mute, captions, high contrast, and low-power as first-class variants in attraction contracts and E2E fixtures.
- Ensure all functionality has keyboard operation, focus remains visible, action targets are comfortably sized, automatic movement can be paused or stopped, and motion-triggered effects have an equivalent input path.
- Give each attraction a comprehension check that asks whether a guest can identify their goal, available action, feedback, and exit regardless of modality.

### 7. Pacing, rest, and controlled intensity

**Sourced facts**

- The micro-break meta-analysis found small but significant average benefits for vigor and fatigue, but no significant overall performance gain (S8).
- S4 identifies physical comfort and fatigue as meaningful queue concerns and warns that play can conflict with bodily needs.

**Inference**

- A 30–60 minute discovery route needs intentionally quiet spaces and permission to pause; the benefit is likely emotional and cognitive recovery, not an excuse to extend the session.

**Project recommendation**

- Interleave higher-attention attractions with low-demand observation or arrangement moments that can be left instantly and resumed without penalty.
- Design a calm central concourse with an understandable return route, predictable motion, and short recap of the guest's accumulated traces.
- Avoid continuous high-arousal audio, scrolling, motion, visual noise, or countdowns. Measure completion and voluntary return separately from time-on-site.

### 8. Finale and exit as reflection and re-entry

**Sourced facts**

- S2 distinguishes remembered pleasure from anticipation and visiting, making post-visit meaning part of the experience model.
- S10 shows that a finale does not erase the contribution of the total route to later memory.
- S11's large review corpus suggests edge friction and unmet expectations can color an otherwise enjoyable visit.

**Inference**

- A personalized finale should make the guest's earlier actions recognizable, then offer a calm, legible exit and optional keepsake. It cannot compensate for confusing navigation, inaccessible interactions, or a weak middle.

**Project recommendation**

- Compose the Midnight Assembly from a small number of clearly attributable motifs earned through the guest's actions; present a short accessible recap naming what changed.
- Do not gate the finale behind exhaustive collection, a score threshold, audio permission, or a hard real-time sequence.
- End with choices that respect closure: replay a realm, view the keepsake, start a different constellation, or leave. Preserve the guest's state locally only with clear explanation and no account requirement.

## Counterexamples and failure modes to carry into concept review

1. **The peak-and-end fallacy.** S10 found that in a complex experience, average valence and arousal outperformed peak/end measures for one-week remembered experience. A dramatic reveal and finale cannot excuse an unrewarding middle.

2. **Decorative agency.** S6's evidence favors choices leading to meaningfully different states. Buttons that merely change dialogue or recolor an interface without any discernible immediate or later consequence risk making the park feel like a guided page rather than a responsive place.

3. **Busywork disguised as anticipation.** S4 and S5 support playful waiting only conditionally. High workload alone did not directly reduce perceived wait in S5, while S4 documents technical and bodily conflict. Mandatory minigames, unclear loading, or inaccessible precision input can turn anticipation into friction.

4. **Sensory maximalism.** S7 did not show a blanket memory advantage from multimodal cues. Excess layers may impair clarity, mute accessibility, low-power performance, or guests' ability to identify what matters. Motif coherence and equivalence matter more than sensory volume.

5. **Operational edges poison the whole visit.** S11's single-resort review corpus identifies access, service, expectation mismatch, and crowd or queue friction as prominent dissatisfaction topics. For ASTERIA, analogous failures include a slow opening, dead-end navigation, loss of state, hidden requirements, or a finale unreachable without unsupported hardware.

6. **Landmark-only navigation.** S3 notes that landmark use depends on person, task, and familiarity. A beautiful but unlabeled skyline cannot replace current-location text, focus order, a return path, and an alternate short route.

## Cross-source recommendation for ASTERIA's concept gate

Adopt the following as testable experience hypotheses, not settled design facts:

1. **Wonder in ten seconds:** lighting the star produces an immediate response and reveals one understandable invitation.
2. **Legible exploration:** a guest can name their location, see a meaningful next destination, and return without external help.
3. **Consequences with receipts:** each attraction leaves an observable local trace and one later trace, with the finale clearly recombining a subset of them.
4. **Anticipation rather than blockage:** travel, loading, and pre-show moments are optional, cancellable, accessible, and stateful; no core progress depends on them.
5. **Breathing room:** the short route reaches a satisfying personal finale without mastery, while the deep route alternates active play and low-demand discovery.
6. **Equivalent authored routes:** mute, reduced-motion, keyboard-only, touch, and low-power guests still perceive the same goal, consequence, and closing meaning.

## Open uncertainties for later research and testing

- No source here directly compares browser-native park arcs against physical-park arcs; transfer must be validated with ASTERIA prototypes, not assumed.
- The cited physical studies cover particular parks, cultures, interfaces, and research designs. Their shared principles are stronger than any proprietary or visual solution.
- The appropriate intensity, attraction duration, audio complexity, and state depth for ASTERIA remain product questions. E1–E5 should test them with observed guests and accessibility variants.
- This record intentionally excludes operator playbooks, fan materials, and visual attraction references so that the concept can remain original by construction.
